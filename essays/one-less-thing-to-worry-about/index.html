One Less Thing to Worry About

Customers may forget the presentation or the price. They remember whether your presence made a difficult moment easier.

Substack3 min read
TrustCustomersLeadership

Over the past few months, I’ve been reflecting on the beliefs that have quietly shaped how I work through customers, colleagues, leadership, and more than a few mistakes along the way.

Here’s one I’ve kept coming back to.

Early in my career, I thought customers remembered the best presentation.

Or the most competitive price.

Or even the project we delivered.

Like many people starting out, I assumed business was won in meeting rooms.

If we explained our solution well enough, submitted a competitive proposal, and delivered what we promised, that was success.

Over time, I realised that wasn’t what customers remembered.

What they remembered were the moments after the contract.

The unexpected phone call.

The missed deadline.

The design change.

The site issue.

The difficult conversation neither side wanted to have.

Those moments reveal far more about a relationship than the presentation ever did.

Winning a contract wasn’t the finish line.

It was simply the moment someone decided to trust us.

The real work started after the signatures.

Would we deliver?

Would we communicate honestly when something didn’t go according to plan?

Would we stay engaged long after the project was complete?

I don’t remember exactly when it happened, but somewhere along the way, one question quietly became my guide.

Can I become one less thing for my customer to worry about?

It sounds simple.

But it changed how I approached almost everything.

It meant taking ownership, even when the answer wasn’t immediately obvious.

It meant calling a customer before they had to call me.

It meant saying, “I don’t know yet, but I’ll find out,” instead of pretending to have all the answers.

It meant staying with a problem until it was resolved rather than until it was someone else’s responsibility.

Looking back, I don’t think customers expected perfection.

No project ever is.

What they wanted was confidence that someone cared as much about the outcome as they did.

Ironically, I think that’s what built the strongest relationships.

Not flawless execution.

Not perfect presentations.

Trust.

I’ve since realised this idea extends well beyond customers.

The best colleagues become one less thing for their teams to worry about.

The best leaders become one less thing for their people to worry about.

The best partners become one less thing for each other to worry about.

The context changes.

The principle doesn’t.

Customers may forget the presentation.

They may forget the proposal.

They may even forget parts of the project.

But they rarely forget how you made them feel when things became difficult.

More than twenty years into my career, that’s still a standard I try to hold myself to.

Not because I always achieve it.

But because I think it’s a goal worth coming back to.

A small note

Over the coming months, I’d like to write more about the beliefs that have quietly shaped how I work. Not because I think they’re universal, but because they’ve stood the test of experience for me.

I’d love to hear which beliefs have stayed with you throughout your own career.
